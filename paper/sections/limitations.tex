\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Simulation only.} Every circuit is evaluated on a statevector simulator,
  cross-checked against PennyLane to $10^{-10}$; nothing here ran on quantum hardware.
  Shot and depolarising noise are modelled by analytic surrogates, whose agreement with a
  density-matrix simulation is measured on a representative subset rather than assumed.
\item \textbf{Small circuits.} On the one-dimensional problems each circuit is one qubit
  without entanglement, a model a classical computer evaluates cheaply; the Helmholtz
  circuits use three qubits. Simulation cost capped the experiments at 6--8 qubits, though
  SMCD's formulas apply unchanged at larger $n$. No claim of quantum computational
  advantage is made or tested.
\item \textbf{Statistical power.} Five seeds per configuration give a smallest two-sided
  Wilcoxon $p$ of 0.0625, so ``confirmed'' means all five seeds agree; bootstrap intervals
  and three alternative tests (Section~\ref{sec:validation}) support each verdict but do
  not raise the sample size.
\item \textbf{Where the method wins, and where it does not.} The eight-copy circuit's
  strongest result is one applied problem. On Poisson a classical model given the same
  frequencies remains $3.3\times$ more accurate, and at high Helmholtz wavenumbers no
  model learns the solution, so parity there says nothing about capability.
\item \textbf{Baseline sizes and tuning.} In the six-problem matrix only the
  frequency-matched model and the random-frequency circuit are size-matched to the
  circuit; the MLP and Fourier-feature network run at standard sizes. The eight-copy model
  was selected from a small grid of copy counts and learning rates, while each classical
  family received a learning-rate search only, and those learning rates did not transfer
  reliably to held-out seeds.
\item \textbf{Coverage.} Achieved coverage does not predict error on its own
  (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}); the coverage sweep builds few distinct circuits, which
  confounds coverage with parameter count.
\item \textbf{The affine encoder.} The classical encoder is restricted to an affine map
  so that the circuit's frequency set, and hence coverage, is well-defined; a nonlinear
  encoder would make $\Omega$ input-dependent. This restricts the hybrid model.
\item \textbf{The predicted-benefit number is a heuristic.} The design card's a-priori
  benefit estimate is not a proven bound. It predicted 2.5\% on heat and zero on
  Helmholtz $k{=}4,10$; the circuit beat the MLP on none of them.
\end{enumerate}
