\section{Application: waterlogging under canal-irrigated farmland}
\label{sec:groundwater}

SMCD needs a solution's frequencies before training. Real problems where those are known
in advance come from regular structure: evenly spaced sources, periodic geometry. This
section applies the method to one such problem, with its claims pre-registered like all
others (Section~\ref{sec:claims}).

\subsection{The problem}
Two rivers $L=2$\,km apart bound a confined aquifer beneath irrigated farmland. Water
enters from background recharge $R_0$ (rain and irrigation return flow) and from six
canals running parallel to the rivers at equal spacing, each leaking $q$ m$^3$/day per
metre of its length into a strip of width $\sigma$. Dupuit's steady one-dimensional
equation for the hydraulic head $h$ (water-table height above the river datum) is
\begin{equation}
  T\,h''(X) = -R(X),\qquad
  R(X) = R_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{6}\frac{q}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(X-c_j)^2/2\sigma^2},
  \qquad h(0)=h_A,\ h(L)=h_B,
\end{equation}
with $c_j=(j-\tfrac12)L/6$. The scenario uses values within textbook ranges
\citep{freeze1979groundwater,todd2005groundwater}, not calibrated to one site:
transmissivity $T=100$\,m$^2$/day (silty sand), $R_0=0.4$\,mm/day, $q=0.4$\,m$^2$/day,
$\sigma=30$\,m, $h_A=1$\,m, $h_B=0$. On $x=X/L$ this is $-u''=f$, the Poisson operator
with non-zero boundary values, so every model and instrument applies unchanged. The
canals fix the frequencies before any training: SMCD's target spectrum is the 32
multiples of $\pi$ up to $32\pi$, and it designs a one-qubit, four-layer circuit that
covers them (Section~\ref{sec:worked}).

Because $\sigma(z\Phi(z)+\phi(z))$ with $z=(X-c)/\sigma$ has second derivative
$\phi(z)/\sigma$, the head has a closed form. An independent second-order
finite-difference solver agrees with it to $5\times10^{-8}$\,m and converges to it at
measured order $2.00$ as the grid is refined from 100 to 6{,}400 intervals
(Figure~\ref{fig:refconv}), which checks the closed form, the forcing and the boundary
treatment together.

\subsection{Where the mound comes from}
The size of the answer can be checked by hand, which also shows which inputs matter. Water
that enters the aquifer between the rivers can only leave through them, so the water table
bulges upward in the middle. For a uniform recharge $R$ between two rivers at the same
level, the equation $T h''=-R$ gives a parabola with a mid-field rise of $RL^2/(8T)$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Background recharge}, $R_0=0.0004$\,m/day:
    $0.0004\times2000^2/(8\times100)=2.0$\,m.
  \item \textbf{Canal seepage}, six canals leaking 0.4\,m$^3$/day per metre, spread over
    the 2{,}000\,m field, is equivalent to $6\times0.4/2000=0.0012$\,m/day of uniform
    recharge: $0.0012\times2000^2/800=6.0$\,m, three times the rain.
  \item \textbf{River levels}, 1\,m and 0\,m, add a straight line that is 0.5\,m at
    mid-field.
\end{itemize}
The total, $2.0+6.0+0.5=8.5$\,m, matches the exact peak of 8.53\,m; the canals' being
narrow strips rather than uniform recharge only adds the small ripples. The same
arithmetic explains the planning answers below. The field waterlogs above 7.5\,m, so it
has 1\,m of headroom over the mound: a 10\% canal lining lowers the canal term by 0.6\,m,
to a peak near 7.9\,m, still above the threshold, while a 20\% lining lowers it by 1.2\,m,
to about 7.3\,m, and the whole field dries. Because the rise scales as $1/T$, clay loam with
a quarter of the transmissivity quadruples the mound and waterlogs most of the field (Figure~\ref{fig:gwlining}).

\subsection{The practical question}
A field is waterlogged where the water table rises within 1.5\,m of the land surface,
into the crop root zone. With the surface 9\,m above the river datum, the water table
peaks at 8.53\,m and 713\,m of the 2\,km field is waterlogged
(Figure~\ref{fig:gwprofile}). The solution answers the planning questions a farmer or
irrigation engineer would ask (Figure~\ref{fig:gwscenarios}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Lining the canals.} Cutting canal seepage by 20\% dries the whole field;
    a 10\% cut reduces the waterlogged length from 713\,m to 494\,m.
  \item \textbf{Soil.} The same canals waterlog 1{,}778\,m on clay loam
    ($T=25$\,m$^2$/day) and none of the field on fine sand ($T\ge150$).
  \item \textbf{Spacing.} Concentrating the same total seepage into two canals
    waterlogs 1{,}066\,m and raises 84\,cm mounds under each canal; spreading it over
    twelve reduces the mounds to 1\,cm.
\end{itemize}

The spacing result is a reminder that the planning answer depends on the fine structure,
not only on the total water: the same seepage from fewer, larger canals builds taller
local mounds that break through the threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:gwspacing}). This fine
structure is exactly the canal ripple that SMCD's frequencies are chosen to capture.

\subsection{Can the trained model answer it?}
The eight-copy SMCD circuit was trained on held-out seeds 10--14 at the full training
budget, against a random-frequency circuit, an MLP and a frequency-matched classical
model all of the same size, and the large MLP (Table~\ref{tab:gw},
Figure~\ref{fig:gwmodels}). Five claims about it were pre-registered, and all five hold on
every seed.

\paragraph{Accurate to millimetres.} The median error is 0.09\%, with every seed below
0.5\%. On a water table that peaks at 8.5\,m, 0.09\% is a typical error of a few
millimetres, far below what any field measurement could resolve. The circuit is
$18\times$ more accurate than an MLP with the same 91 parameters and $9.9\times$ more
accurate than the same circuit with random frequencies, both on every paired seed, and it
is also more accurate than the 8{,}513-parameter MLP, with about 1\% of its parameters.
99.4\% of its improvement over the MLP lies inside $\Omega$, so the canal harmonics SMCD
read off the forcing are where the accuracy comes from.

\paragraph{It answers the practical question.} Relative error is not what a planner
needs; the waterlogged length is. The circuit places it at 714\,m against the exact
713\,m. The same-size MLP, with 1.7\% error, gives 672\,m, 6\% short, because a small
error near the 7.5\,m threshold moves where the water table crosses it. Getting the
planning decision right requires accuracy well below 1\%, and the circuit delivers it.

\paragraph{Why the circuit beats fixed features here.} The classical model handed the
same frequencies reaches 22.9\% error and predicts no waterlogging. A plausible reason,
not tested here, is that the solution is a broad mound plus small canal ripples: a linear
head on fixed sine features at the canal harmonics represents the ripples but not the
mound, while the circuit's trainable encoder can shift and scale its frequencies to fit
both.
