\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Errors are relative $L^2$ against the reference solution, reported as the median over
five seeds. A relative error of 1 is what the trivial prediction $u\equiv0$ scores, so a
model near 1 has learned nothing, and a model above 1 is worse than predicting zero.
Table~\ref{tab:claims} lists every pre-registered claim about the one-copy circuit and
Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} those about the eight-copy circuit; the subsections take the
evidence one question at a time and say what each result means for the method.

\subsection{The circuit builds exactly what SMCD designs}
\label{sec:realise}
For every one-dimensional problem the SMCD circuit was evaluated at 40 random parameter
settings and its output Fourier-transformed. The largest amplitude outside the designed
set $\Omega$, relative to the largest overall, is at most $3.8\times10^{-15}$,
which is floating-point zero, and the test suite checks
the same property on every build. The frequency set could still drift during training,
because the encoder in front of the circuit is trainable; it does not. The encoder drift
$\|A-I\|_F$ at the end of training is 0.13 with one copy and 0.10 with eight, below the
0.2 at which the design card would stop describing the trained model.

\emph{What this means.} Proposition~1 and the ternary depth rule are not only theory: the
design card computed before training is a correct, checkable description of the model
after training. Every later statement about ``inside'' and ``outside'' $\Omega$ rests on
this.


\subsection{Accuracy across six problems}
\label{sec:accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} gives every family's error on every problem with one copy of
the circuit. The six problems fall into three regimes.

\paragraph{Mixed slow and fast waves (Poisson, Burgers, Helmholtz $k{=}4$): classical
models win against one copy.} On Poisson the circuit's error (1.68) is higher than the
MLP's (1.08), the Fourier-feature network's (0.21) and the frequency-matched model's
(0.33), and all three pre-registered comparisons are refuted. Two details sharpen the
reading. The frequency-matched model has the circuit's exact frequencies and 13
parameters to its 14, so the gap ($5.1\times$) is not about which frequencies are
available but about how the model can combine them. And the parallel hybrid, which adds
an MLP beside the circuit, reaches 0.35, close to the frequency-matched model: once the
model has a second, independent path to the output, it trains well. Both are consistent
with the readout of a single circuit being the bottleneck, which
Section~\ref{sec:copyresult} examines and removes.

\paragraph{Smoothing operator (heat): nothing beats a plain MLP, as predicted.} Diffusion
damps each Fourier mode at a rate proportional to $\omega^2$, so the $15\pi$ ripple in the
initial condition vanishes almost immediately and the solution is dominated by a slow
decay that any smooth network represents well. SMCD's design card predicts a benefit of
only 2.5\% here before any training. Every family is worse than the MLP, the circuit by
38\% and the random-frequency circuit by $9\times$, so the negative-control claim (no
family beats the MLP by more than 5\%) is confirmed. The practical rule is that SMCD tells
a user in advance when a quantum layer is not worth adding.

\paragraph{High wavenumber (Helmholtz $k{=}10,20$): every model fails.} Every family's
median error is 0.995--1.09, the score of predicting zero. At these wavenumbers the
forcing term is about 1\% of the Laplacian term, so $u\equiv0$ already leaves a small
residual and gradient descent settles there; this reading is plausible but untested. The
pre-registered trend that the circuit's error ratio against the MLP improves with $k$
holds ($3\times10^{-5}\to0.92\to0.99$), and the circuit is within $1.3\times$ of the
frequency-matched model at both wavenumbers, but these are parities among models that
did not learn the solution and say nothing about capability.

\paragraph{Cost.} On a classical simulator a circuit run takes a median of about
2{,}900\,s against 330--590\,s for the classical families at the same number of steps.
This is the price of simulation, not of the method: on hardware a one-qubit circuit
evaluation is a single short pulse sequence.

\subsection{Designed frequencies beat random ones}
\label{sec:design}
The sharpest test of SMCD changes only one thing: the circuit, its size and its training
stay fixed, and the encoding scalings are either SMCD's or random draws from $U(0.5,3)$
(Figure~\ref{fig:designrandom}). A worked example shows what is at stake. With four
layers, random scalings of at most 3 reach frequencies of at most $4\times3=12$ radians
per unit, while the Poisson and heat solutions contain a $15\pi\approx47$ wave; SMCD's
scalings $\pi\cdot\{1,3,9,27\}$ reach $40\pi\approx126$. The random circuit cannot
represent the fast wave at any parameter setting. Wherever the circuit learns the
solution, the design wins on every seed:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Heat, one copy:} $6.5\times$ lower error on all five seeds, 95\% bootstrap
    interval 2.5--17.7$\times$. The designed circuit is also the most consistent model in
    the whole matrix (its worst and best seeds differ by 4\%), while its random twin is
    the least consistent ($7.4\times$).
  \item \textbf{Poisson, eight copies:} $236\times$ (0.0037 against 0.87), all five
    held-out seeds.
  \item \textbf{Groundwater, eight copies:} $9.9\times$ (0.09\% against 0.92\%), all five
    held-out seeds (Section~\ref{sec:groundwater}).
\end{itemize}
On Poisson with one copy the median ratio is $1.84\times$ but one seed of five disagrees,
so that claim is inconclusive; there the one-copy circuit does not learn the solution
with either scaling.

\emph{What this means.} This is the central claim of the method, and it holds: choosing
the encoding from the PDE's symbol is worth between one and two orders of magnitude in
accuracy over the same circuit with an arbitrary encoding, at no extra cost in parameters.

\subsection{The mechanism in the frequency domain}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\paragraph{The improvement lands on the designed frequencies.} Projecting the error onto
Fourier modes at every checkpoint and accumulating how much better the circuit does than
the MLP at each frequency, 99.9\% of the circuit's total improvement on Poisson lies
inside $\Omega$ with one copy, 99.9\% with eight, and 99.4\% on groundwater; the
pre-registered bar was 70\%. Figure~\ref{fig:heatmaps} shows it directly: the MLP's
error at high $|\omega|$ persists through training (the spectral-bias staircase), while
the circuit clears the rows marked by $\Omega$. On Helmholtz $k{=}10$ the circuit improves
on the MLP at no frequency at all, so there is nothing to attribute.

\emph{What this means.} The circuit's accuracy gains come from exactly the mechanism SMCD
is built on, not from an incidental property such as its size or initialisation.

  \centering

  \caption{Poisson: per-frequency error $|\hat e(\omega)|$ over training, MLP (left,
    \texttt{c\_mlp}) against one copy of the SMCD circuit (right, \texttt{q\_serial}),
    seed 0, with $\Omega$ overlaid as thin white lines.}
  \label{fig:heatmaps}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The NTK is flatter inside $\Omega$ once the model can express it.} Proposition~4
says the quantum block's kernel has non-decaying eigen-directions at the encoded
frequencies, so its spectrum should be flatter inside $\Omega$ than outside. With eight
copies on Poisson the decay exponent inside $\Omega$ is flatter than outside by 15.2
(bar: 0.5). With one copy the test cannot run on Poisson: the model has 14 parameters, so
its NTK has at most 14 non-zero eigenvalues, none beyond index $|\Omega|=81$
(Figure~\ref{fig:ntk}, left). On Helmholtz $k{=}10$, where every model fails, the
spectrum is steeper inside $\Omega$ (gap $-8.4$). The prediction therefore holds exactly
where the model has enough independent directions to show it.

\paragraph{Hybrid models stay close to their initial kernel.} The prediction that hybrids
leave the lazy-training regime earlier is reversed: at 5{,}000 steps the median relative
NTK drift is 1.0 for the one-copy hybrid and $4.3\times10^6$ for the classical models (2.5
against $9.7\times10^4$ with eight copies). The circuits also use few directions in
parameter space throughout: NTK effective rank 1.4 on
Poisson and 1.1 on heat, Fisher effective dimension about 4, both flat over training,
whereas the MLP's effective dimension grows from about 4 to 12 on Poisson and 5 to 50 on
heat. The size-matched classical model behaves like the circuit (effective rank 2.0 and
1.9), so this is an effect of size rather than of being quantum. Near-lazy dynamics are
useful here: a model whose kernel barely moves is well described by its initial NTK, which
is why the design card, computed at initialisation, predicts the trained behaviour.


\paragraph{Coverage alone does not predict error.} Sweeping SMCD's coverage target on
Poisson builds only three distinct circuits (targets of 0.8, 0.9 and 1.0 yield the same
one), so coverage there is confounded with parameter count; at the full training budget
the correlation between coverage and error is $\rho=+0.29$, not the pre-registered
$\rho\le-0.7$. On Helmholtz $k{=}10$ every target builds the same circuit, which scores
about 1 throughout. Covering the band is necessary for the design to help
(Section~\ref{sec:design}) but not sufficient for a single circuit to train well.

\paragraph{No measurable barren plateau at this scale.} Gradient variance fitted against
qubit count (4 and 6 qubits, three seeds) has slope 0.31, under the $\log2=0.69$ bar, but
the fit explains almost none of the variation ($R^2=0.001$): no decay is detectable
between 4 and 6 qubits. SMCD's shallow depths and local observable are designed to avoid
the plateau, and at this scale they do; eight qubits exceeded the simulator's memory.

\subsection{Why one copy is not enough, and what eight copies change}
\label{sec:copyresult}

\paragraph{One copy learns the hard frequency and loses the easy one.} The Poisson
solution is $\sin\pi x+0.3\sin15\pi x$. Tracking the error at those two frequencies
(Figure~\ref{fig:specerr}), one copy cuts its $15\pi$ error to a median 10\% of its
starting value by step 1{,}000, faster than any classical model (Fourier features 103\%,
MLP 86\%, frequency-matched 51\%). Its $\pi$ error falls to about 30\% by step 5{,}000 and
then \emph{rises} to 1.9--2.0$\times$ its starting value by step 20{,}000, on all five
seeds, while the frequency-matched model reduces both.

A worked number explains the trade. The PDE residual contains $u''$, and differentiating
$\sin\omega x$ twice multiplies it by $\omega^2$, so an error at $15\pi$ costs $15^2=225$
times as much loss as the same error at $\pi$. A linear head on fixed sine features sets
the two amplitudes independently, so it can reduce both. A single circuit cannot: all 81
of its Fourier amplitudes are functions of the same ten angles, read out through one
expectation value. Moving the angles to fix the heavily weighted $15\pi$ amplitude moves
the lightly weighted $\pi$ amplitude too, and the optimiser accepts that, because the loss
it saves at $15\pi$ is 225 times larger than what it loses at $\pi$. The loss agrees: it
stalls near $3.3\times10^4$ from step 1{,}000 while the error keeps moving, and the seeds
agree closely (worst over best $1.1\times$) because they all converge to the same
trade-off. This reading is supported by the data but not proven.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{../results/report/figures/xai_spectral_error_poisson.png}
  \caption{Poisson, one copy: error spectrum at each checkpoint (seed 0), with the error at

  \label{fig:specerr}

\paragraph{Copies decouple the amplitudes.} With $K$ copies the head has $K$ independent
weights on $K$ independently parametrised circuits, all with the same $\Omega$. On the
tuning seeds the error falls with the number of copies, from 1.67 with one copy to 0.003
with eight at the higher learning rate (Figure~\ref{fig:copies}): 4 copies already give
0.08, and the drop from one copy to eight is almost three orders of magnitude.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{../results/report/figures/headline_v2_lab.png}

  \label{fig:copies}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Eight copies on held-out seeds.} On seeds 10--14 (Table~\ref{tab:ensemble}) the eight-copy circuit reaches a median error of 0.0037, with
every seed below 0.02: from worse than predicting zero to about 0.4\% error. It beats the
Fourier-feature network by three orders of magnitude and the same circuit with random
frequencies by $236\times$; the NTK is now flatter inside $\Omega$ (gap 15.2), and 99.9\% of
the improvement lies inside $\Omega$. The comparison with the MLP is inconclusive: at its
tuned learning rate the MLP failed on three of five seeds (1.2--3.6) and reached
0.003--0.012 on the other two. The classical learning rates chosen on the tuning seeds did
not transfer well (the Fourier-feature network went from 0.19 to 5.0), which inflates both
ratios, while every circuit seed was stable.

\textbf{The frequency-matched classical model, sized to the eight-copy circuit (83
parameters), is $3.3\times$ more accurate} (0.0011). On Poisson, handing a classical
model the frequencies SMCD reads off the equation works better than the circuit; the
contribution there is the frequency set, which a classical and a quantum model can both
use. The groundwater problem (Section~\ref{sec:groundwater}) is where the circuit's own
trainable encoding makes the difference.
